📜 The rules of the game

Ten rules that govern the whole method. Each step then has two or three of its own, behind the 📜 chip inside that step.

  1. Two variables, one set of cases. Association is defined only for two variables measured on the SAME individuals. Two variables from two different groups cannot be associated — there is nothing to pair up.
  2. Decide the roles before you draw. Exploring whether two things go together → correlation, and neither variable is first. Predicting one from the other → regression, and the explanatory variable goes on x.
  3. The plot comes before the number. Direction, form, strength, outliers — said out loud, in that order. Everything the lecture warns you about is visible in the plot and invisible in the summary.
  4. r measures straightness, and nothing else. r is the strength of the LINEAR relationship. A curve, however perfect, is something r is the wrong instrument for — and it will still print a number.
  5. SP carries the sign; the denominator removes the units and caps r at ±1. r = SP ⁄ √(SS(x)·SS(y)). SS(x) and SS(y) can never be negative. Divide SP by SS(x) instead and you have the slope, not the correlation.
  6. r is symmetric; the line is not. Swap the axes and r is unchanged, but the regression line changes completely. One r, two possible lines — and choosing between them is a scientific decision.
  7. Least squares means squared VERTICAL gaps. The line minimises Σ(y − ŷ)², measured straight up and down, because a vertical gap is a prediction error in y. And (x̄, ȳ) is always on the line — a free check.
  8. A hat means a prediction. ŷ is never an observed value, and a prediction from the line is the predicted AVERAGE for everyone at that x — not a promise about one case.
  9. Neither r nor the line is resistant. A few outlying observations can move both a long way. Odd in y shows up as a big residual; odd in x gives leverage, and leverage is how one case moves the whole line.
  10. Association is not causation, at any strength. Causation, common response and confounding produce identical scatterplots. Which one you are in is settled by the design of the study, never by the size of r.

🎯 Learning objectives

  • State what it means for two variables to be associated, and say why “measured on the same cases” is part of the definition.
  • Decide whether a question calls for correlation or regression, and name the explanatory and response variables when there are any.
  • Place a question on the lecture’s flowchart from the data type and the purpose.
  • Draw a scatterplot by the three rules, and say why one point per case matters.
  • Describe any scatterplot in four words — direction, form, strength, outliers — in context and in one sentence.
  • Recognise a curved form and a hidden categorical variable, and say why neither shows up in r.
  • Explain r as an average product of z-scores, and list its six properties and four cautions.
  • Compute r from a table of five cases: means, deviations, SP, SS(x), SS(y), divide.
  • Fit and interpret a regression line, and make a prediction while saying what the prediction is a prediction OF.
  • State what least squares minimises, and derive b₁ and b₀ from that criterion.
  • Quote the three facts and the four assumptions, and say which variable each assumption is about.
  • Tell an outlier in y from an influential point in x, interpret r² and a residual plot, and name the four ways a well-fitted line can still mislead.

🏛️ The whole method in one paragraph

Start with two quantitative variables measured on the same people, and decide what you are asking: whether they go together (correlation, symmetric) or whether one predicts the other (regression, which needs you to nominate an explanatory variable for the x axis). Draw the scatterplot — one point per case, nothing else — and describe it in four words: direction, form, strength, outliers. If the form is not a line, stop, because every number from here on assumes one. If it is, summarise the strength with r = SP ⁄ √(SS(x)·SS(y)), computed from the means, the deviations and the three sums, and remember that r is unitless, symmetric, capped at ±1 and not resistant. Then turn the same SP into a line: b₁ = SP ⁄ SS(x) = r · s(y) ⁄ s(x), b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄, chosen because together they minimise the squared vertical gaps — and check it by putting x̄ back in and getting ȳ. Interpret the slope in units and the intercept only if x = 0 is inside the data, and predict only inside the observed range. Ask how much the line explained with r², and whether a line was the right shape with the residual plot. Finally, look for the cases that are holding the line up — odd in y means a big residual, odd in x means leverage — and, whatever r turned out to be, do not promote association into causation: causation, common response and confounding all produce exactly the same picture.

🔗 Two variables, and which one explains which

What “associated” actually means, and the question you answer before you draw anything.

⬇ builds oneverything you know about describing ONE variable — mean, SD, histogram⬆ hands upa named pair of variables, with the roles decided and the right cell on the flowchart
  • ▸Two variables measured on the SAME cases are associated if knowing one tells you something about the other you would not otherwise know.
  • ▸“Same cases” is doing real work: two variables from two different groups of people cannot be associated — there is no pairing to look at.
  • ▸A response variable measures an outcome; an explanatory variable explains or causes changes in it.
🪝 memory hookAssociation is a rumour — hearing one variable’s name makes you expect something about the other. Explanatory and response is a job title: someone is doing the explaining, someone is being explained.

In words

Everything so far has been one variable at a time. This lecture takes two at once, and the first job is not arithmetic — it is deciding what you are asking. Two variables measured on the same cases are ASSOCIATED if knowing one tells you something about the other. Sometimes that is all you want to know, and correlation is the tool. Sometimes you want to use one to predict the other, and then you have to nominate which does the predicting — and that is regression.

  • Sometimes there are no roles. If you only want to know whether two things go together, neither is explanatory — and correlation is the right tool.
  • Correlation and regression sit in one cell of the flowchart: a relationship between scale variables, with one predictor.

Rules of the game for step 1

  • Decide BEFORE you plot whether you are exploring or explaining. That decision fixes which variable goes on which axis, and a plot with the axes the wrong way round quietly answers a different question.
  • Nominal data means frequencies and Phi; ordinal means ranks and Spearman’s rₛ. Pearson’s r needs scale data with the distributional assumptions met.
  • “Associated” is a statement about what you can predict, not about what causes what. The lecture’s last slide exists to say so.

Say it to a friend

“Two things are associated if knowing one changes what I’d guess about the other. Sometimes I just want to know they go together — that’s correlation. Sometimes I want to use one to predict the other, and then I have to decide which one does the predicting — that’s regression.”

Key insight

Decide before you plot whether you are exploring or explaining, because that decision fixes which variable goes on which axis — and a scatterplot with the axes the wrong way round quietly answers a different question.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Treating “associated” as a weaker word for “causes”. It is not weaker in the same direction — it is a different claim altogether, and no amount of strength upgrades it.

Where correlation and regression live

Check yourself

A researcher measures anxiety in 40 students and sleep quality in 40 DIFFERENT students, then asks whether the two are associated. What is wrong?
There is no pairing. Association is defined for two variables measured on the SAME cases, so there is no way to place a single dot — you cannot plot a point for a student whose second value belongs to somebody else. The question cannot be asked of these data at all.
When does a pair of variables have no explanatory and no response variable?
When you only want to know whether they go together, with no direction in mind — height and arm span, say. Then correlation is the right tool and the symmetry of r is a feature. The moment you say “predict y from x” you have chosen a direction, and regression is the tool.

📈 Draw the picture

The most useful graph in this chapter, and the three rules for drawing one.

⬇ builds ona pair of quantitative variables with their roles decided⬆ hands upone dot per person — the picture every later step reads
  • ▸One dot per individual. Each case appears exactly once, at the spot its two numbers put it.
  • ▸Step 1 — decide the axes. If a distinction exists, explanatory on x and response on y.
  • ▸Step 2 — label and scale: both axes need a name, units and a sensible range.
🪝 memory hookA scatterplot is a class photo. Every person appears exactly once, standing at the spot their two numbers put them. Summaries come later; first you look at the crowd.

In words

A scatterplot shows two quantitative variables measured on the same individuals, one point per case. There are three rules and nothing else: decide the axes, label and scale them, plot the values. No line yet, no summary, no averaging. It looks like the easy slide of the lecture; it is the slide every later caution depends on, because curves, outliers, influential points and hidden groups are all visible here and invisible in the numbers.

  • Step 3 — plot the values. Nothing else: no line, no summary, no averaging yet.
  • Both variables must be quantitative. A scatterplot of “gender against pack weight” is not a scatterplot.

Rules of the game for step 2

  • One point per CASE, never one point per group. Averaging first and plotting the averages is a different, much tidier and much more misleading picture.
  • The explanatory variable goes on x. r would not care, but the regression line you fit later cares enormously.
  • Draw before you compute. Everything the rest of this lecture warns about — curves, outliers, influence, hidden groups — is visible in the plot and invisible in the numbers.

Say it to a friend

“I put one variable along the bottom and the other up the side, then I mark one dot for each person where their two values meet. If one variable is supposed to explain the other, the explaining one goes along the bottom.”

Key insight

The scatterplot is not an illustration of the analysis — it IS the first step of the analysis. Every caution later in this lecture is a thing you can see in the plot and cannot see in the summary numbers.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Plotting a summary instead of the data — one dot per class, per country, per group. The picture gets tidier and the correlation usually gets stronger, but it is now a statement about groups, not about people.

🧪 Demo 1 · Which dot is which hiker?

Click a name. The point lights up and the reading is spelled out — this is the habit that stops you misreading a scatterplot under exam pressure.

Check yourself

Why does the convention put the explanatory variable on x, when r is the same either way?
Because of what comes next. r is symmetric, so for correlation alone the axes are a matter of habit. But a regression line minimises VERTICAL gaps, so swapping the axes gives a different line and a different prediction. Getting the habit right on the plot stops the mistake later.
A classmate plots “degree subject” against exam mark as a scatterplot. What has gone wrong?
Degree subject is categorical, so it has no position on a number line and the dots can only line up in columns. A scatterplot needs two quantitative variables. The right picture is side-by-side boxplots, and the right analysis compares groups rather than modelling a relationship.

🔍 Read it in four words

Direction, form, strength, outliers — the checklist that answers the exam question.

⬇ builds ona scatterplot drawn the right way round⬆ hands upa sentence you can write in an exam — and a verdict on whether r is allowed at all
  • ▸Direction. Positive: above-average on one goes with above-average on the other. Negative: above goes with below.
  • ▸Form. Linear, curved or clustered — the SHAPE of the cloud, not its tilt.
  • ▸Strength. How closely the points follow that form. Tight to a line = strong; widely scattered = weak.
🪝 memory hookD-F-S-O — Direction, Form, Strength, Outliers. Say them like a checklist at customs: nothing gets through until all four are declared.

In words

Four words describe any scatterplot, and the lecture wants them in order. Direction: which way does it lean? Form: what shape is the cloud? Strength: how tightly do the points follow that shape? Departures: is anybody standing apart from the pattern? Three of the four r can handle. The second one — form — it cannot see at all, and that is why it comes before any number.

  • Departures. An outlier is an individual value falling outside the overall PATTERN of the relationship.
  • Say all four, in context: “a moderately strong, positive, linear relationship between body weight and pack weight, with one possible outlier”.

Rules of the game for step 3

  • D-F-S-O: direction, form, strength, outliers — in that order, every time.
  • Strength is the TIGHTNESS of the band, not its steepness. A steep line through a loose cloud is a weak relationship with a big slope.
  • An outlier here is defined against the PATTERN, not against either variable alone. A point can be unremarkable on x and on y separately and still sit far off the relationship.

Say it to a friend

“I describe a scatterplot the way I’d describe a crowd: which way is it leaning, what shape is it, how tightly packed is it, and is anyone standing on their own?”

Key insight

Form is the one of the four that can cancel the whole analysis. Direction and strength are summaries you can argue about; form decides whether r means anything at all.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Calling a round cloud “no relationship”. A round cloud means no LINEAR relationship. The same picture is produced by genuinely unrelated variables and by a symmetric curve, and only the shape of the cloud tells them apart.

🌙 When the cloud bends, or splits

Two departures from the simple picture — and only the plot shows either.

⬇ builds onthe four words from step 3⬆ hands upa verdict on whether r may be used at all
  • ▸Not every form is a line. Slide 15’s cloud curves upward: the relationship is obvious, and a straight line is the wrong description of it.
  • ▸Curvature is a PLOT-ONLY finding. No summary number announces it.
  • ▸Two clouds can masquerade as one. The tilt of a mixed plot may belong to the GROUPING, not to the relationship inside either group.
🪝 memory hookA curve is a road with a bend — driving straight through it is not a shortcut, it is a crash. A hidden category is a double exposure: two photographs printed on top of each other, and the pattern you “see” belongs to neither.

In words

Two things can go wrong with the form, and neither announces itself in a number. The cloud can bend — slide 15’s relationship is obvious and curved, and a straight line is simply the wrong description of it. Or the cloud can be two clouds: colour the points by some third variable and what looked like one trend turns out to belong to the grouping rather than to the relationship inside either group.

  • Adding a categorical variable: use a different colour or symbol for each category — the slide’s Southern states are the orange dots.
  • That is the visual version of a lurking variable: the third variable is right there in the plot, once you colour it in.

Rules of the game for step 4

  • If the form is not a line, stop. r, b₁ and r² will all still compute, and all three will be answering a question about a straight line the data do not follow.
  • Before accepting a correlation, ask what third variable could be hiding in the cloud, and whether colouring by it would split the picture in two.
  • A curve is not “a weak relationship”. It can be an almost perfect relationship that r is simply the wrong instrument for.

Say it to a friend

“Sometimes the dots make a curve instead of a line, and then the correlation number is the wrong tool entirely. And sometimes the dots are really two separate groups — colour them in and the story changes completely.”

Key insight

The same scatterplot can hide two opposite stories: one relationship bent into a curve, or two separate groups sitting at different heights. Both produce a number that looks respectable and means nothing.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reporting r for a curved cloud “because the question asked for r”. If the form is not linear, the honest answer names that and says r is not an appropriate summary.

Check yourself

A plot of exam score against hours studied rises steeply at first and then flattens off. What do you report, and what do you not report?
You report a strong but CURVED positive relationship — the gain per extra hour falls as hours rise. You do not offer r as its strength, because r measures only closeness to a straight line and will understate a relationship this clear. Here the plot is the finding.
Two countries are pooled in one scatterplot and the cloud tilts upward, yet within each country the relationship is flat. What is going on?
The tilt belongs to the grouping, not to the relationship. One country sits higher on both variables, so pooling manufactures a trend that exists in neither. Colouring by country reveals two flat clouds at different heights — the visual form of a lurking variable.

🧲 What r measures

A product of two z-scores — and the six properties and four cautions that follow.

⬇ builds ona plot whose form you have confirmed is a line⬆ hands upone number for strength and direction, with its cautions attached
  • ▸r measures the strength of the LINEAR relationship between two quantitative variables. Strength means closeness to a straight line.
  • ▸Matching corners are positive: above average on both, or below on both. Mismatched corners subtract. r is the net result across everybody.
  • ▸Standardising removes the units — dividing by s(x) and s(y) turns pounds and points into “how many SDs from the mean”.
🪝 memory hookr is an agreement score. Each person votes “we agree” (both above average, or both below) or “we disagree” (one of each), and the size of their vote is how extreme they are. r is the average vote.

In words

The slide is blunt about why a number is needed at all: our eyes are not always good judges of how strong a relationship is. So r supplements the plot — it never replaces it. What r does is turn every person into two z-scores and multiply them. Agree with the trend and the product is positive; disagree and it is negative. r is the average of those votes, and standardising is what strips out the units.

  • r is always between −1 and +1; ±1 only for a perfect straight line; near 0 means a very weak LINEAR relationship.
  • Our eyes are not reliable judges of strength, which is the whole reason the lecture introduces a number at this point.

Rules of the game for step 5

  • r makes no distinction between explanatory and response. Swap the axes and r is unchanged.
  • r has no units and does not change if you change the units of x, y or both — so it is comparable across studies in a way a slope is not, unless the two happen to use identical units.
  • r is not resistant. The eight hikers give r = 0.795; drop the 187-pound hiker and it is 0.946. One case in eight moved it by 0.15.

Say it to a friend

“For every person I check whether they’re above or below average on each variable. If people who are high on one tend to be high on the other, the products are positive and r is positive. r is basically the average of those agreements, scaled so it can never leave −1 to +1.”

Key insight

r is a thermometer for straightness. It reads high only if the points line up — it cannot tell you the room is on fire in some other shape.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading r as a percentage. r = 0.5 does not mean “half”. The share of variation is r², which is 0.25 — and the gap between the two numbers is widest exactly where students quote them.

Check yourself

Body weight is re-recorded in kilograms instead of pounds. What happens to r, and what happens to the slope?
r is unchanged — every z-score is unaffected, because the deviations and the SD shrink by the same factor. The slope DOES change, because it carries the units: pack pounds per kilogram is a different number from pack pounds per pound. That asymmetry is the practical difference between the two measures.
Which is the stronger relationship, r = −0.82 or r = +0.61?
r = −0.82. Strength is distance from zero; the sign only gives direction. The negative one is both the stronger and the opposite in direction, and a full answer says both.

🧮 Compute r by hand

Slide 28’s table, column by column — SP, SS(x) and SS(y).

⬇ builds onthe definition of r as an average of agreements⬆ hands upSP, SS(x) and SS(y) — the three sums the slope will reuse
  • ▸Means first: MX = 13, MY = 6. Every other column is measured from these.
  • ▸Deviations next, and each deviation column must sum to zero — a free check on your arithmetic.
  • ▸SP is the product column: Σ(X − MX)(Y − MY) = 40. The only column where the two variables meet.
🪝 memory hookSP is the couples’ dance, SS is each partner practising alone. r asks how much of the solo energy actually showed up in the dance.

In words

Five cases, two juries, four columns. Take the means first, because every other column is measured from them. Then the deviations, which must each sum to zero. Then multiply the two deviations together for each case and add: that is SP, the only column where the two variables meet. Square each deviation separately and add: those are the two SS. Divide and you have r.

  • SS(x) and SS(y) are the squared columns, 44 and 40 — each variable’s own variability.
  • Divide: r = 40 ⁄ √(44 × 40) = 40 ⁄ 41.95 = 0.953.

Rules of the game for step 6

  • Check that each deviation column sums to zero before going further. It costs five seconds and catches most arithmetic slips.
  • SP is the only one of the three sums that can be negative. SS(x) and SS(y) are sums of squares, so the sign of r is entirely the sign of SP.
  • Dividing SP by SS(x) instead of by the root of the product gives 0.909 — that is the SLOPE, not the correlation.

Say it to a friend

“I subtract each mean, multiply the two deviations for each person and add them up — that’s SP. Then I square each deviation separately and add those up — those are the SS. r is SP divided by the square root of the two SS multiplied together.”

Key insight

r = 0.953 here does NOT mean the two juries agree. Jury 2 scores everyone far lower than jury 1. r sees only each case’s distance from its OWN mean, in SDs, so the difference in level is invisible to it.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Forgetting the square root, or taking it of only one sum. The denominator is √(SS(x) · SS(y)); √44 × 40 and √(44 × 40) are very different numbers, and only one of them keeps r inside ±1.

🧪 Demo 4 · The table, live

Change any number and watch the deviations, SP, SS and r recompute. Three presets are waiting: the lecturer's jury data, a perfect straight line, and a deliberately useless one.

Check yourself

A table gives SP = 60, SS(x) = 100 and SS(y) = 144. Give r, and give the slope.
r = 60 ⁄ √(100 × 144) = 60 ⁄ 120 = 0.50. The slope is b₁ = SP ⁄ SS(x) = 60 ⁄ 100 = 0.60. Same numerator, different denominator: r is unitless and capped at ±1, the slope carries units and is not capped at all.
Your deviation column for x sums to −2 rather than 0. What does that tell you?
That either the mean or at least one deviation is wrong — deviations from a mean sum to exactly zero by construction. Recompute MX and the column before touching SP, because every sum downstream inherits the error.

📐 Fit the line: b₀, b₁ and a prediction

Correlation treats both variables equally. Regression does not — and that buys prediction.

⬇ builds onSP and SS(x) from step 6⬆ hands upŷ = b₀ + b₁x — a machine you can put an x into
  • ▸Regression has an independent (predictor) variable X and a dependent (outcome) variable Y. Correlation has neither.
  • ▸b₁ is the slope — the amount by which y changes for each one-unit increase in x.
  • ▸b₀ is the intercept — the value of y at x = 0, which is often outside the data and meaningless on its own.
🪝 memory hookb₀ is the starting salary and b₁ is the annual raise. The line tells you what you earn after x years — and nobody should ask about year −5.

In words

Here the symmetry breaks. Regression nominates an independent variable X and a dependent variable Y, and the line describes how y changes as x changes. That asymmetry costs you something — swap the axes and you get a different line — and buys you something r can never give: put a value of x in, get a predicted y out.

  • ŷ is a PREDICTION, never an observation. The hat is the whole distinction.
  • A regression line describes how y changes as x changes, and lets you predict y for a given x.

Rules of the game for step 7

  • Interpret the slope in the units of both variables, and say “predicted”. “Each extra percentage point of adults returning predicts about 0.30 FEWER new birds” is the full answer.
  • Regressing y on x and x on y give DIFFERENT lines. Only r is symmetric.
  • A prediction from the line is the predicted AVERAGE for everyone at that x, never a promise about one case.

Say it to a friend

“The regression line is a recipe: start at b₀ and add b₁ for every unit of x. Put a value of x in, get a predicted y out. Unlike correlation, it matters enormously which variable I chose to predict.”

Key insight

The sign of the slope and the sign of r are always the same, because b₁ = r · (s(y) ⁄ s(x)) and both SDs are positive. They can never disagree — if yours do, one of them is wrong.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Reading the slope backwards. “0.30 fewer new birds per percentage point returning” and “0.30 percent fewer returning per new bird” describe different lines, and only one of them is the line you fitted.

Why is b₀ = 31.93 not “the number of new birds when none return”?
Arithmetically it is exactly that — but x = 0 means a colony where no adult returns at all, far outside anything observed and probably not a colony. b₀ is the number that sets the height of the line; it is interpretable only when x = 0 sits inside the data.

🟥 Least squares — why this line and no other

“Least squares” names exactly what is minimised. Say the phrase in full and the formulas follow.

⬇ builds onthe idea of a line drawn through the cloud⬆ hands upthe formulas for b₁ and b₀, and a reason to trust them
  • ▸Squares, not distances. Squaring makes every gap positive and punishes big misses far more than small ones.
  • ▸Vertical, not perpendicular — because the quantity we are wrong about is y.
  • ▸“Least” is literal: no other straight line gives a smaller total for this dataset.
🪝 memory hookThe least-squares line is the laziest possible compromise: it cannot please everyone, so it settles where the total squared complaint is smallest.

In words

Of all the straight lines you could draw, least squares picks one by a single criterion: measure how far each point sits above or below the line, square those gaps, add them up, and take the line where that total is smallest. Vertical, because the quantity we are wrong about is y. Squared, because the plain sum is zero for the best line and for many terrible ones.

  • b₁ = SP ⁄ SS(x) = r · (s(y) ⁄ s(x)) — the slope is the correlation, rescaled into the units of the two variables.
  • b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄, which forces the line through the point (x̄, ȳ).

Rules of the game for step 8

  • Say the phrase in full — “the line that minimises the sum of the SQUARED VERTICAL distances” — and each formula stops needing to be memorised separately.
  • The plain sum of residuals is useless as a criterion: it is zero for the best line and for many terrible ones. That is why we square first.
  • Check every fitted line by putting x̄ back in. You must get ȳ.

Say it to a friend

“For any line I could draw, measure how far each point sits above or below it, square those gaps and add them up. The least-squares line is the one where that total is smallest — and there’s a formula, so I never have to guess.”

Key insight

b₁ = r · (s(y) ⁄ s(x)) says the slope is the correlation wearing units. Standardise both variables and the slope IS r — which is also why a correlation can never be read as a rate of change.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Measuring the gaps perpendicular to the line because they look shorter. Least squares is about prediction error in y, and a prediction error is vertical by definition.

b₁ = r · (s(y) ⁄ s(x)) = 0.40 × (15 ⁄ 5) = 1.20. Reversing the ratio gives 0.13 and forgetting to multiply by r gives 3.00 — both common, both wrong.
Which point is guaranteed to lie on every least-squares line, and why?
(x̄, ȳ), the point of the two means. It follows directly from b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄: substitute x̄ into ŷ = b₀ + b₁x and ȳ comes back out. It is the fastest check you have on a fitted line.

🪜 Three facts, four assumptions

Slides 38 and 39 — the most quotable lines in the lecture.

⬇ builds ona fitted least-squares line⬆ hands upwhat the line can be trusted to say, and the conditions it says it under
  • ▸Fact 1. A change of one SD in x corresponds to a change of r SDs in y. With r = 0.5, a whole SD above average on x predicts only HALF an SD above average on y.
  • ▸Fact 2. The least-squares line always passes through (x̄, ȳ).
  • ▸Fact 3. The explanatory/response distinction is essential — regressing y on x and x on y give different lines.
🪝 memory hookFact 1 is a tax on prediction. You arrive one SD above average, the line takes its cut, and you are credited with only r SDs. The weaker the relationship, the higher the tax.

In words

Three facts and four assumptions, and between them they contain most of the lecture’s exam marks. Fact 1 is where the word “regression” comes from: a change of one SD in x buys only r SDs in y, so predictions always fall back towards the mean. The four assumptions are stated here but tested nowhere — they are what Lecture 5 will need in order to put a p-value on anything.

  • The four assumptions: a linear relation; y normally distributed at each value of x; the variance of y the same at each value of x; independent observations.
  • Nothing is assumed about the distribution of x. That is the most-tested line on the slide.

Rules of the game for step 9

  • Fact 1 is where the word “regression” comes from: predictions always fall back towards the mean, and the weaker the relationship the further back they fall.
  • The assumptions concern y AT EACH VALUE OF x, not y overall. A histogram of y is not the check.
  • Lecture 4 states the assumptions and tests nothing with them. They start doing work in Lecture 5, where they are what makes a p-value mean something.

Say it to a friend

“If someone is one standard deviation above average on the predictor, I don’t predict they’ll be one SD above on the outcome — only r of an SD. Predictions always fall back towards the mean, and that’s where the word regression comes from.”

Key insight

Regression to the mean is not a force acting on people; it is arithmetic. Any imperfect predictor MUST give predictions less extreme than its input, or it would be claiming more accuracy than it has.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Hearing “regression to the mean” as a claim that extreme cases become average over time. It is a statement about PREDICTIONS from an imperfect predictor, not a prophecy about the cases themselves.

Check yourself

A selection test correlates r = 0.50 with later job performance. A candidate scores 2 SDs above the mean. What performance does the line predict?
1 SD above the mean: a change of one SD in x corresponds to r SDs in y, so 2 × 0.50 = 1. Predictions are always less extreme than the input unless r = ±1, and the gap is the test admitting how much it does not know.
Which of the four assumptions is about x?
None of them. Linearity is about the relationship; normality and equal variance are about y AT EACH VALUE of x; independence is about the observations. Nothing at all is assumed about how x is distributed — “x must be normal” is the classic wrong answer.

🧲 Outliers, and the ones that move the line

The Gesell data: one child takes r² from 41% to 11%.

⬇ builds ona fitted line and the residuals it leaves⬆ hands upa judgement on whether one case is holding the whole line up
  • ▸An outlier is an observation lying outside the overall pattern of the other observations.
  • ▸Outliers in the y direction have large residuals — far above or below the line. Child 19 is one.
  • ▸Outliers in the x direction are often INFLUENTIAL: removing them would markedly change the equation. Child 18, at 42 months, is one.
🪝 memory hookAn outlier in y is a shout — loud, obvious, easy to spot. An outlier in x is a lever — quiet, and it moves the whole room.

In words

A point can be odd in two different ways, and the lecture wants you to tell them apart. Odd on the outcome means a large residual: it sits far above or below the line and is easy to spot. Odd on the predictor means leverage: it sits far out in x, at the end of a long lever, and if its y is also off the trend it swings the whole line towards itself.

  • Influence needs leverage. Far out in x is the end of a long lever; if the y is also off the trend, the line swings towards it.
  • Removing child 18 takes r² from 41% down to 11%. “Not resistant” is not an abstraction.

Rules of the game for step 10

  • Odd in y is loud (a big residual); odd in x is dangerous (leverage). The exam asks you to tell them apart.
  • A strongly influential point can have a SMALL residual, because it has already dragged the line over to meet it. Never judge influence by the residual alone.
  • Refit without the point and compare. That comparison is the definition of influence, and no single number replaces it.

Say it to a friend

“A point can be odd in two ways. Odd on the outcome means it’s far from the line. Odd on the predictor means it can swing the line towards itself — and then it stops looking odd at all, which is why I always refit without it and compare.”

Key insight

Far out in x and ON the trend of the rest gives leverage without influence — it steadies the line rather than bending it. Leverage is the opportunity to move the line; influence is taking it.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Deleting an inconvenient point. Nothing in the data says an extreme value is an error. You report the analysis with and without it, and let the reader see the difference.

🟢 r² and the residual plot

How much did the line explain — and was a line the right shape at all?

⬇ builds ona line you have checked for influential points⬆ hands upa number for how much, and a verdict on the shape
  • ▸r² is a fraction of the variation, between 0 and 1, usually quoted as a percentage.
  • ▸It is literally r squared, so r = 0.30 explains 9% — and the SIGN disappears, because a negative relationship explains just as much as a positive one.
  • ▸A residual is observed y − predicted y: the vertical distance from a point to the line.
🪝 memory hookr² is the score; the residual plot is the referee. A high score with the referee waving a flag is not a result.

In words

Two diagnostics that answer completely different questions, and students confuse them constantly. r² says how much of the variation in y the line accounts for — a share, usually quoted as a percentage. The residual plot says something more basic: was a straight line the right shape at all? A curved relationship can score respectably on the first and fail the second.

  • A residual plot puts the residuals against x. Ideally, random scatter around zero.
  • Residual patterns suggest deviations from a linear relationship — a curve means the wrong shape, however high r² looked.

Rules of the game for step 11

  • r² answers HOW MUCH. The residual plot answers WHETHER A LINE WAS THE RIGHT SHAPE. They can disagree, and the exam likes the case where they do.
  • r² is a share of the VARIATION, not of the cases. “40% of the variation” is right; “right for 40% of people” is not.
  • A positive residual means the point sits ABOVE the line. Observed minus predicted, in that order, every time.

Say it to a friend

“r² tells me what share of the differences in the outcome my predictor accounts for — 40% means 60% is still down to something else. Then I look at the leftovers: if they’re random noise, a line was fine; if they make a pattern, I fitted the wrong shape.”

Key insight

Squaring destroys the sign, which is why r² can never tell you the direction. Quote r² for how much and r for which way — and never one in place of the other.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Computing predicted minus observed and calling it a residual. The order matters: a hiker carrying 30 lb when the line predicts 26.5 has a residual of +3.5, and the sign is how you know they are above the line.

Two residual plots, side by side

Check yourself

A study reports r = −0.60. What percentage of the variation in y does the regression explain?
36%. r² = (−0.60)² = 0.36, and the sign disappears on squaring, so −0.60 explains exactly as much as +0.60 — it just explains it in the opposite direction. “−36%” is not a thing.
r² = 0.78 and the residual plot shows a clear curved band. What do you report?
That a straight line is the wrong model despite the high r². The curve in the leftovers is shape the line failed to capture, so b₁, r² and every prediction are answering a question about a form the data do not follow. The residual plot outranks r² here.

🚧 The four ways a good line still lies

Sarah at thirty, and the three diagrams that close the lecture.

⬇ builds oneverything: a plot, a line, a fit and a residual check⬆ hands upthe sentence you are allowed to write — and the four you are not
  • ▸Extrapolation is predicting y for an x outside the observed range. Sarah’s line gives 88 cm at 42 months — sensible — and 209.8 cm at 360 months.
  • ▸Lurking variables have an important effect on the relationship but are not included in the study.
  • ▸Association, however strong, does NOT imply causation. The lecture’s closing line, and the exam’s favourite.
🪝 memory hookExtrapolation is reading past the end of the map. The map is not wrong — it simply stops, and the line keeps drawing anyway.

In words

The line can be perfectly fitted, pass every check, and still mislead you in four ways. You can ask it about an x it has never seen. A lurking variable can be driving both of your variables. The relationship can be real and the arrow point the other way, or nowhere. And one odd case can have shaped the whole thing. The lecture closes on the fourth: association, however strong, does not imply causation.

  • Both correlation and regression describe LINEAR relationships, and both are affected by outliers. Always plot the data before interpreting.
  • The three causal diagrams — causation, common response, confounding — all produce exactly the same scatterplot.

Rules of the game for step 12

  • Predict only inside the range of x that was observed. Outside it the model has no evidence at all, and the line keeps drawing anyway.
  • Causation needs a design, not a bigger r — and random assignment is the cleanest evidence there is.
  • When you see a correlation, name a plausible lurking variable out loud. If you can name one, this association ON ITS OWN cannot support “causes”.

Say it to a friend

“The line only knows the range of x it was built from. Outside that it’s making things up. And even inside it, a relationship can exist because x causes y, because something else causes both, or because the two are tangled together — and the data look identical in all three cases.”

Key insight

Causation, common response and confounding are not degrees of certainty — they are different arrangements of arrows, and the data cannot tell them apart. Which one you are in is a question about the study design, not about the numbers.

The mistake almost everyone makes

Treating a very large r as evidence of causation. Strength says how tight the band is; it says nothing at all about where the arrows point, and the ice-cream and drowning correlation is a strong one.

Common response. Hot weather causes both independently, and no plausible arrow runs between ice cream and drowning. Confounding would need ice-cream sales to plausibly affect drownings as well, so that the two effects could not be separated — here only one arrangement of arrows is credible.

  • d.(x̄, ȳ)
Fact 2 on slide 39. It follows directly from b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄: substitute x̄ into the equation and ȳ comes back out.
The other options
✗a. The line passes through the origin only if b₀ happens to be zero, which is rare.
✗b. The largest x is often the point with the most leverage, but nothing guarantees it sits on the line.
✗c. Nothing in least squares uses the median; every formula here is built on means.
🏷️ Put x̄ into your fitted line. If ȳ does not come out, the arithmetic is wrong.
Question 11 · step 7 · fit the line
For the colonies, ŷ = 31.9343 − 0.3040x. How should the slope be interpreted?
  • a.Colonies lose about 30% of their birds in the course of each year
  • b.30.4% of the variation in new birds is explained by the returning adults
  • c.Each extra percentage point of adults returning predicts about 0.30 fewer new birds
  • d.Each extra new bird predicts about 0.30 percent fewer adults returning
The slope is the change in the PREDICTED response per one-unit rise in the explanatory variable, and here it is negative.
The other options
✗a. The slope is a rate of change between two variables, not a percentage of anything lost.
✗b. That is r², a different quantity entirely — and a slope of −0.3040 tells you nothing at all about its value.
✗d. That reading would require regressing x on y — a different line with a different slope.
🏷️ Say the units out loud: “new birds per percentage point”. If the sentence does not carry both, it is the wrong reading.
Question 12 · step 7 · fit the line
Using ŷ = 31.9343 − 0.3040x, how many new birds are predicted for a colony with 60% of adults returning?
  • a.19.17
  • b.13.69
  • c.18.24
  • d.31.93
31.9343 − (0.3040)(60) = 31.9343 − 18.24 = 13.69 birds — slide 47. Note that this predicts the average for colonies at 60%, not the count for one particular colony.
The other options
✗a. 19.17 is what you get by putting 42 into the line — the 42 months from the Gesell and Sarah examples, not a percentage from this one.
✗c. 18.24 is the slope term on its own, with the intercept forgotten.
✗d. 31.93 is b₀ — the prediction at x = 0, not at x = 60.

Question 13 · step 11 · r² and residuals
A study reports r = −0.60. What percentage of the variation in y is explained by the least-squares regression of y on x?
  • a.60%
  • b.−36%
  • c.77%
  • d.36%
r² = (−0.60)² = 0.36. The sign disappears when you square, so a correlation of −0.60 explains exactly as much as +0.60.
The other options
✗a. 60% is r itself read as a percentage — the commonest slip in this lecture.
✗b. A share of variation cannot be negative; squaring removes the sign.
✗c. 77% is √0.60 — the square root rather than the square.
🏷️ r for direction, r² for how much. Never quote one in place of the other.
Question 14 · step 11 · r² and residuals
A hiker’s observed pack weight is 30 lb and the line predicts 26.5 lb. What is the residual?
  • a.56.5
  • b.+3.5
  • c.−3.5
  • d.3.5%
residual = observed y − predicted y = 30 − 26.5 = +3.5 lb. A positive residual means the point lies above the line.
The other options
✗a. 56.5 adds the two numbers instead of subtracting.
✗c. −3.5 is predicted minus observed — the subtraction the wrong way round, which also reverses what the sign tells you.
✗d. A residual is in the units of y, here pounds. It is not a percentage.
🏷️ Observed minus predicted, in that order. Positive means above the line.
Question 15 · step 11 · r² and residuals
A residual plot shows a clear curved band rather than random scatter. What does this tell you?
  • a.A straight line is the wrong model for these data
  • b.The correlation for these data must be close to zero
  • c.There is an influential outlier somewhere in the x direction
  • d.The residuals must have been calculated incorrectly
Ideally residuals scatter randomly around zero; a pattern suggests a deviation from a linear relationship. Note that r² can still look respectable while the residual plot says the shape is wrong.
The other options
✗b. Not necessarily — a curved relationship can still produce a respectable r and r².
✗c. An influential point is one odd case, not a pattern running through every point.
✗d. A systematic curve is a real feature of the data and survives any correct recomputation.
🏷️ A curve means the wrong SHAPE; a fan means unequal spread; one stray point means an outlier.
Question 16 · step 10 · outliers and influence
Removing child 18 from the Gesell data changes r² from 41% to 11%. Child 18 is best described as …
  • a.a lurking variable affecting both age and score
  • b.a data-entry error that should be deleted from the dataset
  • c.an influential observation, because removing it markedly changes the line
  • d.an outlier in the y direction, because it has a very large residual
Child 18 is extreme in x (42 months), which gives leverage. Outliers in y have large residuals; outliers in x are often influential.
The other options
✗a. A lurking variable is a third variable outside the study, not one of the observations in it.
✗b. Nothing in the data says the value is an error, and removing a point because it is inconvenient is not legitimate.
✗d. Outliers in y have large residuals. An influential point often has a SMALL one, because the line has already swung to meet it.
🏷️ Odd in y → big residual. Odd in x → leverage, and possibly influence. Refit without it to find out which.
Question 17 · step 12 · the four warnings
Sarah’s line ŷ = 71.95 + 0.383x fits her heights between 36 and 60 months. Predicting her height at 360 months gives 209.8 cm. The problem is …
  • a.that r² was too small for any prediction to have been made
  • b.extrapolation — 360 months is far outside the range of x that was observed
  • c.that the line was fitted by least squares rather than by eye
  • d.that height and age are not linearly related at any age at all
The model is fine inside the observed range — at 42 months it gives a sensible 88 cm. The error is asking it about an x value 300 months beyond anything it has seen.
The other options
✗a. r² for Sarah’s toddler data is very high. A good fit inside the range is exactly what makes the extrapolation tempting.
✗c. Least squares is the correct method; the fitting is not what went wrong.
✗d. Height and age ARE close to linear over 36–60 months, which is why the line fits so well there.

Question 18 · step 12 · the four warnings
Ice-cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly correlated across the year. The best description is …
  • a.causation — buying ice cream genuinely increases the risk of drowning
  • b.confounding — the effect of ice cream cannot be separated from the weather
  • c.a calculation error, since a correlation like this cannot be real
  • d.common response — hot weather drives both, with no arrow between them
In common response a lurking variable z — here, hot weather — causes both x and y independently. Confounding would require ice-cream sales to plausibly affect drownings as well, which they do not.
The other options
✗a. No plausible mechanism runs from ice cream to drowning; the arrow is not there.
✗b. Confounding needs the two effects to be tangled — that is, x must plausibly affect y as well. Here only the weather is doing anything.
✗c. The correlation is perfectly real. What is wrong is the causal reading, not the arithmetic.
🏷️ Common response: z → x and z → y, no arrow between x and y. Confounding: z → y AND x → y, tangled together.
Question 19 · step 9 · facts and assumptions
Which of these is NOT an assumption of least-squares regression as the lecture states them?
  • a.the variance of y is the same at each value of x
  • b.the observations are independent of one another
  • c.x must be normally distributed
  • d.the relation between x and y is linear
Slide 38 lists a linear relation, Y normally distributed at each value of X, equal variance of Y at each X, and independent observations. Nothing is assumed about the distribution of x itself.
The other options
✗a. Equal variance of y at each x — homoscedasticity — is the third.
✗b. Independence is the fourth.
✗d. Linearity is the first of the four.
🏷️ Every assumption is about y, or about the relationship, or about the observations. None is about x.
Question 20 · step 9 · facts and assumptions
A selection test correlates r = 0.50 with later job performance. A candidate scores 2 SDs above the mean. What performance does the regression predict?
  • a.1 SD above the mean
  • b.2 SDs above the mean
  • c.0.5 SDs above the mean
  • d.4 SDs above the mean
Fact 1: a change of one SD in x corresponds to a change of r SDs in y. So 2 × 0.50 = 1 SD. Predictions are always less extreme than the input unless r = ±1 — this is what “regression” originally meant.
The other options
✗b. That would require r = 1, a perfect predictor.
✗c. 0.5 SDs is r itself, with the candidate’s score of 2 SDs forgotten.
✗d. 4 SDs divides by r instead of multiplying — it makes the prediction MORE extreme than the input, which no imperfect predictor can do.
🏷️ Multiply the input by r. The answer must always be closer to the mean than the input was.
Question 21 · step 3 · read it in four words
A point in a scatterplot sits comfortably inside the range of x and inside the range of y — neither value is unusual on its own — and yet it counts as a possible outlier. How can that be?
  • a.Any point more than two standard deviations from the mean of x is counted as an outlier
  • b.An outlier is defined against the PATTERN of the relationship, not against either variable on its own
  • c.The lecture is using the word loosely here; strictly speaking the point is not one
  • d.A point must be extreme on at least one of the two variables, so this has to be an error
The lecture’s definition is “an individual value that falls outside the overall PATTERN of the relationship”. A point can sit inside the range of x and inside the range of y and still be far off the relationship joining them — which is exactly why you look at the scatterplot rather than at two separate histograms.
The other options
✗a. No such cut-off appears anywhere in this lecture. Outliers here are judged against the pattern, by eye.
✗c. The definition is explicit, and this point satisfies it. The lecture is being precise rather than loose.
✗d. A point can be unremarkable on x and on y separately and still sit far off the relationship — that is the whole reason for a two-variable picture.
🏷️ Two tidy histograms can hide an obvious stray in the scatterplot. Judge an outlier against the pattern.
